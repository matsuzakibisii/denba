Is “Weaker Legs and Hips Is Just About Age” Really True? What Recent Insights Teach Us About the Muscles Behind Your Stride and Fall Prevention

Published: June 28, 2026  |  Source published: June 27, 2026

“I've gotten slower on my feet lately,” or “I tire more easily than before” — it's easy to write changes like these off as “just age.” But in June 2026, a health lecture hosted by an internal medicine clinic drew attention by suggesting that changes in walking ability may involve more than age — muscles stiffened by everyday posture and habits may also play a role. Here's an easy-to-follow summary for readers in midlife and beyond.

Three Points the Lecture Makes

  • The “iliopsoas,” which connects the spine, pelvis, and hip joint, is noted to stiffen easily from long hours of sitting. This muscle is involved in swinging the leg forward and maintaining posture; when it stiffens, it can contribute to a hunched back, shorter stride, and fatigue, the lecture explains.
  • The “gluteus medius,” on the side of the hip, is described as playing a role in single-leg stance, walking stability, and balance. When this muscle weakens, unsteadiness and a slower walking pace can become more noticeable, the lecture notes.
  • Stiffened muscles should first be “loosened” rather than immediately strengthened, the lecture explains. Moving them slowly and broadly under light load, to restore their natural range of motion, is described as the foundation for any strengthening exercise that follows.

What We, in Midlife, Should Keep in Mind

This lecture isn't saying that all changes in the legs and hips can be fixed with strength training alone — it's a reminder to look beyond age as the default explanation, and consider how accumulated sitting habits and posture may be affecting your walking ability. The following commonly recommended habits are said to help maintain walking ability.

  • After long periods of sitting, take time to gently stretch the hip flexors and glutes
  • Bring balance-focused movements, such as standing on one leg, into your daily routine
  • Use changes in your stride length or walking speed as a gauge for checking in on your overall condition

* The above are commonly recommended general health habits and do not guarantee improvement or treatment of any specific condition.
